## The short of it

**Where the reviewer was right and the documents must change (6 things):**
sellers will not create records for future provenance — they want paying this
week, so the repair bench and the free ID service become the main record-makers,
not seller submissions. Neutrality can never be proven, only made *testable*.
Cut Drops and cut Auction from the ladder. Free ID needs a boundary or it eats
every week. And A3's index must be able to print a *lower* number than 2016, or
it is marketing — that one is fatal if unfixed, and I gave three mechanisms for
it, one of which costs you something real.

**Where the reviewer was also wrong.** He knocked down "data before venue" with
StockX, Bring a Trailer and Reverb, and then proposed "trust before liquidity"
instead. But his own three examples share one thing he missed: **somebody else
had already given those objects their identity.** Nike supplies the sneaker's
SKU. The state supplies the car's VIN. The guitar has a serial and a price guide
printed every year since 1990. That is why those venues could open first.

Discogs had to build record identity because no label published it. PCGS had to
build the condition grade because no mint supplied it.

**A pipe has neither.** No serial, no agreed condition standard, shape numbers
that changed meaning quietly across decades. That is Discogs and PCGS at once,
and it is the strongest version of your case — not "data comes first" as a rule,
but "nobody else is giving pipes an identity, so whoever does owns the category."
Your cabinets and the dating engine already are that.

## The one thing nobody asked, and it is the big one

The reviewer called the seller incentive the weakest assumption. It is not. The
weakest is the one underneath it, which he accepted and so did the document:
**that a record lasts.** A record only lasts if we can recognise the same pipe
five years later. Pipes have no serial numbers. That leaves the Grain Print —
and we have never measured whether it works. Nobody has published a false-match
rate for it, and grain changes with staining, oxidation, sanding and re-waxing.
A refinished pipe is exactly the pipe most likely to come back through a dealer's
hands.

If the Grain Print does not hold up, four things fall together: the permanent
object page, the logbook, the gap-costs-money incentive, and the repeat-sales
index in A3.

**This is a LOCAL job and I would put it above everything else in this portfolio.**
Take the pipes you have genuinely bought back a second time — especially any that
were refinished in between — and test whether the Grain Print recognises them.
That is a measurement, not an argument, and it decides whether the registry is
buildable at all. If the answer is poor there are fallbacks (a physical mark, an
owner-held certificate, human-assisted matching) — but finding out afterwards
would be expensive.

Second measurement, also local: **what would it cost to get sold-price data that
isn't ours?** The honest index has to exclude your own sales, or every critic
calls it a mirror and they will be right. That means buying or gathering
observations from auctions and other dealers. Nobody has priced it. If it turns
out too expensive, the honest move is to publish the CPI line only and say why —
better than a mirror.

## One correction to the reviewer's comfortable ending

He said the likely outcome is "everyone references us, nobody sells through us,"
and that this is fine. I agree it is the likely outcome. I do not agree it is
comfortable. **Being the reference is a position, not an income.** The money still
has to come from the hands: restoration, your own inventory, consignment — and
one line that is not built yet and that I think is the best thing in either file:
**documenting living collections.** Every other line in the business gets harder
as the collectors age. That one gets bigger. It is also the moment a man trusts
you most. It deserves its own file when you want it.
