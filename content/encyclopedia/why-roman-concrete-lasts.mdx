The Pantheon's dome has stood, unreinforced, since around 128 AD. Many modern harbour walls built with Portland cement begin to fail within decades. The difference is not luck — it is chemistry.

## The recipe

Roman builders combined three ingredients: **quicklime**, **volcanic ash** (*pozzolana*, quarried near Pozzuoli on the Bay of Naples), and water — seawater, for marine structures. Vitruvius describes the proportions in *De Architectura*, and archaeological cores from harbours at Caesarea and Baiae confirm the ancient texts.

The pozzolanic reaction produces calcium-aluminium-silicate-hydrate, a binding mineral that continues to develop for centuries. Where modern concrete is essentially inert once cured, Roman concrete kept *curing*.

## The self-healing accident

For years, the white **lime clasts** peppered through Roman concrete were dismissed as poor mixing. Research published in 2023 by teams at MIT and Harvard suggested the opposite: the clasts appear to be a designed feature, produced by "hot mixing" with quicklime.

- A crack forms and water enters
- The water reaches a lime clast and dissolves it
- The calcium-rich solution recrystallises as calcite
- The crack seals itself

## What we took from it

Modern "self-healing" concretes — some using bacteria, some using encapsulated minerals — are attempts to engineer deliberately what the Romans achieved with local materials and empirical craft. Several research groups now produce Roman-inspired mixes with measurably longer marine lifespans and a smaller carbon footprint than Portland cement.

*Historians believe the Romans understood the durability of their mix through observation rather than theory — Vitruvius records the "what," not the "why." The chemistry had to wait nineteen centuries for an explanation.*
