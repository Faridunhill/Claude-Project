No factory documented itself like Alfred Dunhill's. From the 1910s onward, Dunhill stamped its pipes with a system of patent numbers and date codes that — read correctly — can date a pipe to a single production year. That precision is why Dunhill dating is the reference standard collectors learn first.

## The finishes

Identifying the finish narrows the era before you touch a date code:

- **Bruyere** — the original smooth dark-red finish, produced from the beginning
- **Shell Briar** — the deep craggy sandblast, patented in the late 1910s
- **Root Briar** — light smooth finish introduced in the early 1930s
- **Tanshell, Redbark, Cumberland, County, Amber Root** and others followed in later decades — the presence of a finish name alone sets an earliest-possible date

## The eras, broadly

**Patent era (1910s–mid 1950s).** Pipes carry patent numbers ("PAT. No…"). The patent number itself, plus small suffix digits near the stamping, encode the production year. Patent-era pipes are the most sought-after by collectors.

**Post-patent transition (mid 1950s–1970s).** Patent numbers disappear; a suffix number following the "MADE IN ENGLAND" stamp encodes the year of manufacture.

**Modern era (1970s onward).** The suffix-digit convention continues in evolved forms, joined by white-spot stem logos and standardized shape/size group numbers.

## How the date suffix works

On most post-1950s Dunhills, look immediately after "MADE IN ENGLAND" for a small digit or pair of digits set slightly apart or raised. That digit, added to the base year of the era's convention, gives the production year. The convention changed several times, which is why the same "2" can mean different years on different pipes — the surrounding stamping style tells you which convention applies.

## Size and shape groups

The number inside a circle (or the leading digit of the shape code) indicates the size group — from the smallest group 1 up through the larger group 5 and 6 pipes. The remaining digits encode the shape. Catalogue cross-reference confirms the pairing.

## Caveats every collector should know

- **Replacement stems** are common on estate Dunhills — a correct-era stem matters to value.
- **Worn stampings** can silently drop the date suffix; an "undatable" Dunhill often just has a worn final digit.
- **The system has exceptions.** ODA/ODB shapes, gold-band models, and transition-year pipes need case-by-case reading.
