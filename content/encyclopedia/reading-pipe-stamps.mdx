Every factory pipe carries its story stamped into the briar. The nomenclature — the maker's marks pressed into the shank — is the single most important evidence for identifying and dating a pipe. Before anything else, learn to read it.

## Where to look

- **Left side of the shank** — traditionally the brand name and model line
- **Right side of the shank** — country of origin, shape numbers, finish names
- **Underside of the shank and bowl heel** — shape numbers, size codes, date codes on some makers
- **The stem** — brand logos, inlaid dots, stamped or inlaid lettering

## What the marks tell you

**Brand and line.** The main stamp identifies the maker, and often the specific line — a finish name like "Shell," "Rustic," or "Bruyère" narrows the identification considerably.

**Country of origin.** "Made in England," "Made in France," "Italy" — origin stamps changed wording over the decades, and those changes are dateable. A pipe stamped "England" versus "Made in England" can belong to different eras of the same factory.

**Shape numbers.** Most factories used numeric shape codes. Cross-referencing the number against period catalogues identifies the exact shape, size, and sometimes the year range it was produced.

**Date codes.** A minority of makers — most famously Dunhill — used explicit dating systems: patent numbers, suffix digits, and underlined numerals that can date a pipe to a single year. Where they exist, date codes turn identification into arithmetic.

## Reading a faint stamp

Decades of handling wear nomenclature down. Before giving up on a faint stamp:

1. **Raking light.** Hold the pipe under a single light source at a low angle and rotate it slowly — worn impressions catch shadow.
2. **Photograph, don't squint.** A sharp macro photograph, slightly underexposed, often reveals more than the naked eye.
3. **Transcribe the fragments.** Even partial letters matter. "—NHILL" and "—ADE IN ENG—" are enough to reconstruct a full stamping when compared against reference examples.

## When the stamps are gone

An unstamped or worn-smooth pipe is not unidentifiable. Shape language, stem logic, drilling style, and finish are all attributable — this is where comparative visual analysis against a reference collection does the work stamps no longer can.
